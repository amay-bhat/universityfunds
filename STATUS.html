Judgement calls made along the way

Five questions were too consequential to answer casually and too specific to guess at. Each was escalated to a stronger model for an independent answer, and each is recorded in full — the question, the options, both recommendations, and what would justify revisiting it.

Yale stopped publishing its mix after FY2020. Fill the gap or leave it? Leave it

Percentages could have been derived from Yale’s financial statements, but that table excludes cash and directly-held bonds — so the derived mix would have handed a reader a “Yale portfolio” with almost no bonds, for a school that holds roughly 30% in them. A footnote cannot repair a number that points the wrong way.

Harvard reports one combined equity line where the schema splits US from international. Store it as published

Added one coarser category rather than splitting Harvard’s figure with a guessed ratio, or merging the categories for everyone. Merging would have left no honest benchmark before 2009, when no cheap global equity fund yet existed — degrading Yale’s good data to accommodate Harvard’s coarser reporting.

Six Harvard tables carry no date. Which fiscal year do they belong to? Settled with evidence

Rather than ruling on it, each undated table was reconciled against Harvard’s audited statements. FY2021 was decisive on its own: the 34% private-equity figure did not exist at the start of that year and did at the end. Guessing by publication year would have shifted six years of the series by one, invisibly.

Yale reports a negative cash weight in some years. Is that a typo to clean up? Real, so keep it

It is genuine borrowing — the portfolio was leveraged. Clamping it to zero or folding it into another category would misstate both. The validator now accepts negatives, warns on every one, and requires the school’s own wording for the line.

Two hedge-fund and private-equity benchmark series have no honest source. Explicit gap — decided

No freely-citable, actually-investable series reaches back to FY2000, and these two categories are about half of Yale’s portfolio every year. Settled in task 1.7 with your confirmation: the copycat models only what an ordinary investor can actually buy and shows these slices as a labelled gap. The size of that gap is itself one of the honest results the site will show.

A school publishes percentages for its investment pool, not the endowment itself. Usable? Only under strict conditions

Yes if the school itself presented the pool as its endowment proxy, at least 95% of the endowment’s money is documented as inside the pool, nothing shows the two diverging, and the basis is labelled everywhere including on the chart. MIT’s three early years qualify — and only merged after a second session independently re-fetched the source and matched every digit. Stanford’s fail two of the four tests, so its gap stands.
